# Editorial and visual direction

## Purpose before page count

Use a page brief: **reader question → evidence/story → best visual → useful next action, if any**. Delete a section when its purpose duplicates a neighbour. A route overview and a detailed neighbourhood map have different jobs; three repeated packing summaries do not.

Useful rhythms include a photographic opener, a readable route overview, destination chapters, focused object stories, discreet stay/food choices and practical reference pages. This is a menu, not a mandatory sequence.

## Voice

Start with a particular thing. “A ring of roots now carries the canopy” opens a botanical story; “This is perfect for nature-loving parents” is sales copy. Explain what happened and why it is visible here. Use sourced dates, names and details selectively. Avoid unearned superlatives and generic descriptions that fit any city.

Let practical information be direct. Let narrative passages breathe. Short sentences are not a substitute for substance; long articles should develop ideas rather than repeat travel encouragement. Do not invent scenes of personally being there. Distinguish reported experience from your editorial recommendation.

## Imagery and rights

Prefer actual place, room and food images that survive a useful crop. Track creator, source page, licence/permission, attribution and modifications. Public access to an image is not reuse permission. Public promotional use requires a fresh rights review. Do not remove watermarks or present commercial property imagery as licensed merely because it is official.

Use one strong hero image rather than a grid of tiny thumbnails. Smaller pictures should reveal different evidence: a bedroom, a courtyard, a specific object. Captions identify what is visible and, where meaningful, why it matters. Generated conceptual art must be labelled and cannot stand in for documentary evidence.

## Maps and infographics

- City orientation: river, neighbourhood clusters, major transit, public anchors.
- Daily movement: sequence, modes, approximate distance/time, a readable legend.
- Timeline: causal history or scheduling conflicts, not a decorative row of dates.
- Budget comparison: same basket, units, subtotal and uncertainty.
- Stay comparison: real trade-offs, not invented numerical taste scores.

Use a real basemap when geographic detail helps. Mapbox/deck.gl are options, not requirements; use a suitable open-data static map when simpler. Respect tile licences, provider attribution and offline/export terms. No secret token in distributable HTML. Every print map must remain useful without interaction. Use approximate pins where appropriate and label estimated timings. QR codes must have a clear action and a visible text link beside them.

## Readability and composition

Start with a warm or neutral paper background, a restrained palette drawn from the destination and one editorial serif paired with a legible sans. These are optional defaults, not a fixed brand. Prefer generous whitespace, short line lengths and consistent alignment over ornamental boxes. Body copy generally needs 11–13 pt at final print size; enlarge for the reader, and test rather than trusting CSS units.

Use alternating image/essay/map rhythms. Keep page furniture quiet and repeated headers minimal. Do not split headings from text, crop faces or signs accidentally, or conceal overflow. On mobile, reflow columns and comparison tables. Maintain contrast and descriptive alt text.

## Editor's pass

Ask of every spread: What does the reader learn? What evidence supports it? Does the image prove or reveal something? Is a chart easier than a sentence? Is this advice already elsewhere? Does the plan respect the reader's taste without continually explaining that it does?

Cut generic reflection prompts, repeated reassurance, forced itinerary exercises, unexplained ratings and decorative illustration. Strengthen weak sections with specific material or remove them. The best page count is the one the material earns.
